# The Reading Rail

## Problem

Parker may read hundreds of briefs in a day. A brief needs a small number of sections, each short enough to scan and clear enough to stand alone. The sections should stay visible at the left as he moves through the page. A record of how long each section or entry was actually on screen should travel with the saved HTML, so the next writer can see what was passed over. A short view can mean the material was irrelevant or that its presentation failed to draw attention; the record cannot decide which.

## Success measure

In a trial of twenty real briefs, Parker can locate and act on the section he needs within three minutes for at least sixteen of them. This is a proposed threshold, not a measured baseline. Record the time from opening a brief to the relevant note, stamp, or decision, then ask him whether the saved visibility record helped the next revision. Do not interpret time on screen as comprehension.

## What the reader sees

On a wide screen, a compact list of section names stays on the left while the document moves. The current section is marked. Each item shows a small visible-time value. An entry that the active reader never reached shows `0:00`; a brief with no measurement record shows a dash. On a narrow screen, the list takes a compact form above the document so it does not crowd the words. The reading surface keeps its existing comments and Copy map.

## Announcement

Brief sections now stay in view as you read. Each section shows the time it was visible while you were active on the page. The saved brief carries that record alongside your notes. When a section received little time, the next writer can inspect it and ask whether it was irrelevant or hard to notice. The brief itself is shorter and gives each section enough context to make sense on its own.
